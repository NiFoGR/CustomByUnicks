Where stone and tile bathrooms go wrong

The material is rarely the problem. A fine marble or a convincing stone-effect porcelain can look cheap within a week of fitting if there is a thin sliver of tile at one wall, the edges catch the light at different heights, or the grout lines drift off level.

Most of these faults are decided before the first tile goes down. They come from a wall that was never flattened, a timber floor that flexes underfoot, or a layout that started in a corner because it was quicker. Once the adhesive has set, none of it can be corrected without taking the tiles off again.

What poor tiling costs you later

Uneven large tiles are not only a visual issue. A raised edge on a floor catches toes and chips easily. Hollow spots behind a tile, where adhesive coverage was patchy, leave voids that can crack under load and let water sit behind the surface.

In a shower, failed grout and silicone are the usual route for water into the wall or floor, and from there into ceilings and joists. Natural stone adds its own risks. An unsealed marble can absorb oils and stain, a light marble can show grey shadows from the wrong adhesive, and an acidic cleaner can leave dull etch marks on a polished face.

Putting any of this right means stripping and replacing, often with a stone you can no longer match. That is why the preparation matters more than the price per square metre of the tile.

Natural marble or stone-effect porcelain: an honest comparison

Both can make an excellent bathroom. The right choice depends on how you use the room and how much care you are happy to give it.

Natural marble

Real marble has a depth and variation that no printed surface fully matches, and it ages with character. It is also softer and more porous than porcelain. It needs an impregnating sealer before grouting and resealing from time to time, and it reacts with acids, so lemon juice, some toiletries and many bathroom cleaners will etch a polished finish.

A honed finish hides etching better and gives more grip when wet. Material and fitting usually cost more than porcelain, because slabs need careful selection and handling, and often a specific adhesive.

Large-format stone-effect porcelain

Good porcelain is dense, hard-wearing and resistant to staining, and most glazed porcelain needs no sealing at all. It comes in very large panels, so walls can have few joints, and veining can be sequenced so neighbouring pieces read as one continuous slab.

The trade-offs are real. The pattern repeats across a batch, the cut edge of a glazed tile shows a different body colour, and very large panels need two fitters, special handling and a very flat background.

Many of our rooms combine the two: porcelain where water and wear are highest, natural stone for a vanity top, a trough basin or a floor inlay where you see and touch it most. We bring samples of both to the survey, so you can judge them in your own light.

Setting out so every cut is balanced

Before any adhesive is mixed, we plan the layout on a drawing and then mark it on the walls and floor. The aim is simple: equal cuts at both ends of a wall, no slivers at a ceiling, window reveal or shower tray, and joints that line up from floor to wall where the design calls for it.

  • We find the centre lines of the room and of key features such as a niche, a vanity or a mirror, and work outwards from them.
  • We move the start point so the smallest cut is generous, ideally at least half a tile where the room allows.
  • We size niches, shelves and bench tops to the tile module, so they are wrapped in full or matched pieces rather than trimmed to fit.
  • With book-matched or sequenced faces, we lay the pieces out dry first and number them, so the veining runs where you agreed it should.

This is slow work, and it is where most of the visual quality of a stone room comes from. If you are also planning a walk-in shower, agree the tile module at the same time as the screen and drain position, so all three line up.

Large formats: flatness, lippage and the background

Lippage is the height difference between neighbouring tile edges. With small tiles it barely shows. With a large porcelain panel or a marble slab, low light from a window or an LED strip will pick out the smallest step. Three things control it.

A flat, stiff, prepared background

Large tiles cannot follow a wavy wall or floor, so the background must be flat before tiling starts. Old plaster is often taken back and replaced with cement-based tile backer board, which also copes with the weight of stone. Floors are checked for level and flatness and corrected with a levelling compound where needed.

Many older homes in [City] have suspended timber floors, and here we look closely at deflection. A floor that bounces will crack grout and stone, so it is stiffened and overboarded, with an uncoupling membrane where the design needs one. Natural stone tolerates movement less well than porcelain, so it needs an even stiffer floor.

Full adhesive coverage

We use a notched trowel sized to the tile, back-butter large pieces and lift tiles during the job to check coverage. BS 5385, the British Standard for wall and floor tiling, calls for solid bedding in wet areas, and voids under a large format are a common cause of cracking. Light marbles go down on white adhesive so grey does not show through, and some moisture-sensitive stones need a rapid-setting or resin adhesive to stop them curling.

Bond pattern and levelling

Long rectangular tiles have a slight natural bow along their length. Laying them in a half bond puts high and low points side by side, so we usually offset them by a third or less. A levelling clip system then holds the edges in plane while the adhesive cures.

Grout, silicone, mitred edges and trims

Joints and edges are what you look at from close range, so they are chosen as carefully as the tile.

  • Grout. Rectified porcelain and calibrated marble allow narrow joints, but never zero joints, because every tile needs a small gap to absorb movement. A stain-resistant cement-based grout suits most walls. Epoxy grout resists staining in showers, but needs skilled application and is not right for every stone, so we test it on a spare piece first.
  • Silicone. Internal corners, changes of plane and the junction with a tray or bath are movement joints, not grout lines. We seal them with a sanitary silicone and, on natural stone, a neutral-cure type that will not stain or etch the surface.
  • Mitred edges. External corners on niches, reveals and boxing can be mitred, so two pieces meet at a fine 45 degree corner and the stone appears to wrap around. We leave a tiny bevel on the arris so it does not chip. On glazed porcelain a mitre shows the body colour, so we choose tiles whose body suits it.
  • Trims. A slim profile in brushed brass or stainless steel protects an edge in a busy spot and can be the better choice for tiles that do not mitre cleanly. It is a design decision, not a shortcut.

Bespoke stone inlays and the compass-star floor

An inlay is where stonework becomes something closer to joinery. The compass-star floor in the room above is a single design cut from contrasting marbles, assembled so every point meets exactly, then framed with a dark marble border that sets the proportions of the room.

Pieces like this are waterjet cut from a drawing made to your room's dimensions, and usually arrive as a panel ready to lay. Every stone in it must be the same thickness, or the floor is ground flat after laying. Polished marble can be slippery when wet, so where feet will be wet we discuss a honed finish. Fabrication typically adds 3 to 4 weeks to the programme, which we plan in from the start.

Inlays also depend on how they are lit. A low, grazing light brings out texture and veining, and also reveals any unevenness, so the floor and the bathroom lighting are designed together.

Stone & tiling: common questions

Is marble a good choice for a bathroom?

Yes, if you are happy to look after it. Marble is durable enough for floors and walls, but it is porous and reacts with acids. It needs sealing when fitted and resealing from time to time, and it should be cleaned with a pH-neutral cleaner. A honed finish hides small etch marks and is less slippery when wet. If you want a low-maintenance room, stone-effect porcelain in the shower with real marble elsewhere is a sensible balance.

Does porcelain look as good as real marble?

Good large-format porcelain can be very convincing, especially in big panels with sequenced veining that runs from piece to piece. Up close, natural marble has more depth and every slab is unique, while porcelain patterns repeat across a batch. Porcelain is harder, stain-resistant and usually needs no sealing. The best way to decide is to see both side by side in your own bathroom light.

How often does a marble bathroom need resealing?

There is no single interval, because it depends on the stone, the finish and how much water and product it sees. Shower floors need attention sooner than a vanity wall. A simple test is to put a few drops of water on the surface: if they soak in and darken the stone within a few minutes, it is time to reseal. We leave you care instructions for the specific stone and sealer used.

Can large-format tiles go on a timber floor?

Yes, provided the floor is stiff enough and properly prepared. Timber floors flex, and large tiles and natural stone do not tolerate that movement, so the joists are checked, the floor is stiffened and overboarded, and an uncoupling membrane is often added. Skipping this step is a common reason for cracked tiles and grout on upstairs bathroom floors.

What is lippage in tiling?

Lippage is a difference in height between the edges of neighbouring tiles. It is most visible on large formats, polished surfaces and anywhere light skims across the wall or floor. It is controlled by a flat background, full adhesive coverage, a sensible bond pattern for long tiles and a levelling clip system during fitting. Once the adhesive has set, it cannot be corrected without lifting the tile.

Should I choose mitred tile edges or metal trims?

Mitred edges give the cleanest look, with the stone appearing to wrap around a corner, and suit niches, reveals and boxing. They take more time and need a tile whose body colour looks right when cut. Metal trims are tougher on corners that get knocked and suit tiles that do not mitre well. A brushed brass trim can also be a deliberate design detail.

How do I clean a marble bathroom without damaging it?

Use warm water and a pH-neutral stone cleaner, and dry the surface with a soft cloth where you can. Avoid vinegar, lemon, bleach, limescale removers and most general bathroom sprays, because acids and harsh chemicals will etch or dull marble. Wipe up toiletries and hair products promptly, and use a squeegee in the shower to limit water marks.
