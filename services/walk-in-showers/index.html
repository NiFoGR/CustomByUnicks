Why so many walk-in showers disappoint

A walk-in shower looks simple: a sheet of glass, a floor and a drain. That simplicity is exactly why faults show. There is no door or curtain to hide behind, so a shallow fall, a weak rain head or a screen in the wrong place is obvious every time you use it.

The complaints are consistent. Water creeps across the floor beyond the glass. The drain cannot keep up, so you stand in a puddle. The rain shower looked generous in the showroom but delivers a trickle at home. Silicone around the tray turns black, and behind it a slow leak works its way into the floor.

What a failing shower actually costs

A leaking shower does its damage out of sight. Water that gets past cracked grout or a failed silicone joint soaks into plasterboard, timber and screed long before you see a mark. By the time a stain appears on the ceiling below, or tiles start to sound hollow, the fix is rarely a reseal. It usually means stripping the shower back to the structure and starting again.

Poor performance costs you in a different way. A rain head on a supply that cannot feed it, or a valve that swings from hot to cold when someone runs a tap, is not something you can tile your way out of. Both are decided at the planning stage, which is why we look at your water supply before we talk about finishes.

Shower tray or tiled floor

There are two sound ways to build a walk-in shower floor, and both can look excellent. The right one depends on your floor structure, your drainage route and the look you want.

A low-profile tray

A good low-profile tray is factory-made with its fall built in, so drainage is predictable. It suits most homes, especially upstairs on timber floors, and it is quicker to install. The tray must sit on a fully supported, level base, not on dabs of adhesive over boards that flex.

Very slim trays need care. Some ultra-shallow wastes hold less water in the trap than Building Regulations guidance expects for a shower, which can let drain smells into the room. We check the waste as carefully as the tray, and plan how it will be reached for cleaning.

A tiled, level-access floor

A tiled floor lets the same stone or porcelain run from the door into the shower, with no visible tray edge. It needs a former or screed laid to a consistent fall towards the drain, a tanking system across the whole wet zone, and tiles cut to follow that fall. On timber floors we stiffen the structure first, because movement is what cracks grout and breaks a waterproof seal. Our wet room service covers this build in more depth.

Linear drains suit large-format tiles because the floor only needs to fall in one direction. Centre drains need the floor cut into four planes, which works better with smaller tiles or mosaic.

Water pressure and choosing the right valve

The shower you can have depends on the water your home delivers. Homes in [City] run on every kind of system, so at survey we identify yours and measure the flow at the outlets before recommending a shower.

  • Combi boiler: mains pressure, but a limited flow of hot water. A large rain head and a hand shower running together may be more than it can heat.
  • Unvented cylinder: mains pressure with a store of hot water. Generally the best match for rain showers and multiple outlets.
  • Gravity-fed system: low pressure from a tank in the loft. A shower pump can help, but it must draw from stored water rather than the mains and suit the cylinder and pipework.

We fit thermostatic valves as standard. They hold the temperature you set when other taps are used and shut off if the cold supply fails, which protects against scalding. Concealed valves are built into the wall with a planned access route, so cartridges can be serviced without breaking tiles.

Where you want a ceiling rain shower, we choose one your supply can actually feed, rather than one that only looks right in a brochure. If your system cannot support it, we tell you before the quote, not after the tiles are on.

Screens that keep water where it belongs

A walk-in screen is fixed glass with an open entry, so it keeps water in through geometry rather than a door. The length of the glass, the position of the rain head and the direction the floor falls together decide how much water reaches the rest of the room.

We set the rain head well back from the opening, run the fall away from the entry and size the glass for your room rather than for a catalogue. Screens are toughened safety glass, fixed into wall channels or brackets with a stabilising bar, and can have an easy-clean coating. A brass or black frame adds a crisp line, but it is the fixing and the fall that keep the floor dry.

Built-in benches and lit niches

A stone bench and a recessed niche are what make a shower feel built rather than bought. They are also where shortcuts show first, because every edge and joint sits in the wettest part of the room.

  • Benches are built on a rigid, waterproof structure, tanked with the rest of the shower and topped in stone with a slight fall so water runs off rather than sitting.
  • Niches are set out to the tile grid so the joints line through, with a sloped sill so they drain.
  • We form niches within stud walls or a built-out section, rather than cutting into external or structural walls.
  • LED strips under a bench or inside a niche are rated for their electrical zone, with drivers placed outside the wet area where they can be reached, and the work notified under Part P.

Light in these places is about more than mood. A softly lit bench edge helps you judge a step on a wet floor at night. Our bathroom lighting page explains how we plan light around the shower.

Questions

Walk-in showers: common questions

Is a walk-in shower the same as a wet room?

Not quite. A walk-in shower has an open entry and a fixed glass screen, usually on a tray or a defined tiled area. In a wet room, the whole floor is waterproofed and drains to the shower, so there is no separate enclosure. A walk-in shower is often the simpler and more contained option, while a wet room suits rooms where you want one continuous floor.

Do I need a shower tray for a walk-in shower?

No. You can have a low-profile tray or a tiled floor laid to a fall towards a drain. A tray is quicker, predictable and suits most timber floors. A tiled floor gives a continuous stone or porcelain finish with no tray edge, but needs a stiff floor, a former or screed, and full tanking. We recommend one or the other after checking your floor at survey.

Will a rain shower work with my combi boiler?

Often, yes, but within limits. A combi delivers mains pressure, but it can only heat a certain flow of water at once. A modest rain head on its own usually works well. A large head running alongside a hand shower or body jets may run cooler or weaker than you expect. We measure your flow at survey and match the shower to it.

Do walk-in showers splash outside the glass?

A well-planned one should keep the floor beyond the glass largely dry in normal use. That depends on the length of the screen, where the shower head sits and which way the floor falls. Problems come from short screens with the head close to the opening. We plan those three things together, and tell you if your room needs a longer screen or a different head position.

How do I look after a natural stone shower bench?

Natural stone is sealed after installation and needs resealing periodically, depending on the stone and how often it is used. Clean with a pH-neutral cleaner made for stone, and avoid acidic or bleach-based products, which can etch marble. A quick squeegee after showering reduces water marks and limescale. We give you written care guidance for the specific stone we install.

How long does a walk-in shower installation take?

Replacing a shower area within an existing bathroom typically takes 1 to 2 weeks on site. A full shower room with new drainage, a tiled level floor, a built-in bench and natural stone will take longer, because each layer needs time to cure before the next goes on. We give you a programme with your quote so you know what happens when.
