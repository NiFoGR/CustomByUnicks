What goes wrong with wet rooms

A wet room has no shower tray. The whole floor becomes the tray, so every part of it has to drain and stay watertight for as long as the room exists. That is why badly built wet rooms fail more visibly than any other kind of bathroom.

The usual faults are predictable. Water pools in a corner because the floor was not laid to a consistent fall. Waterproofing was applied around the shower only, so spray that reaches the rest of the floor soaks into the boards beneath. A timber floor flexes, the membrane splits at a joint, and nobody knows until a stain appears on the ceiling below.

Others fail on comfort rather than leaks: a floor that is slippery when wet, towels that are always damp because there is no screen, or a rain shower that delivers a trickle because nobody checked the water supply.

Why getting it wrong costs so much

When a shower tray leaks, you replace the tray. When a wet room leaks, the repair usually means lifting the whole floor, drying or replacing the structure underneath, renewing the waterproofing and laying the floor again. Matching the original tiles or stone years later is rarely possible.

Slow leaks are the worst kind. Water that soaks into timber joists for months can cause rot, and damp inside a floor or wall is a reliable source of mould. A badly built wet room can also raise questions when you sell, because a surveyor will look closely at any staining below it.

Done properly, a wet room is one of the most practical rooms in a house: easy to clean, level underfoot for every age, and open in a way that does not date like a bulky tray and enclosure can.

How we build a wet room that stays watertight

We design and build your wet room as one team, so the layout, drainage and finishes are planned together before work begins. The sequence is fixed, and nothing is covered up until it has been checked.

  1. Survey. We check the floor construction, the depth and direction of the joists, the route and distance to the soil pipe, and your water pressure and flow.
  2. Design. We position the drain, shower, bench, niches and glass so water goes where it should, then confirm everything in a fixed written quote.
  3. Floor build. The floor is stiffened and a wet room former or screed is laid to a consistent fall towards the drain.
  4. Waterproofing. The floor and walls are tanked as one continuous layer, bonded into the drain, with reinforced corners and sealed pipe penetrations.
  5. Checks. The membrane and drain are inspected and tested with water before a single tile is laid.
  6. Finishes. Stone or porcelain, lighting, brassware and glass are fitted, natural stone is sealed and every movement joint is finished with flexible sealant.

Falls, drains and floors: the detail that matters

A consistent fall

The floor in the showering area must fall gently and evenly to the drain: steep enough to clear water, shallow enough that you barely feel it underfoot. The fall has to be built into the floor itself, not created with extra adhesive under the tiles, which leaves hollow spots and uneven edges.

Linear or centre drain

A linear drain is a long, narrow channel, usually set against a wall or across the shower area. It lets the floor fall in a single direction, which suits large-format tiles and stone slabs because they can be laid without being cut into sections. A centre drain needs the floor to fall from all four sides, which means diagonal cuts running to the drain. It works best with smaller tiles or mosaics, which follow the slopes more easily.

Timber floors

On an upstairs timber floor, the wet room area is usually lowered between the joists and a pre-formed former is fitted, so the finished floor stays level with the landing. Whether that is possible depends on the depth and direction of your joists, and joists must never be cut in a way that weakens them. Where the floor cannot be lowered, a low-profile walk-in shower can give a similar look with less structural work.

Tanking the whole room

Tiles and grout let water through. The real waterproof layer is the membrane beneath them, and in a wet room it covers the entire floor, the shower walls to full height and the remaining walls to a safe height above the floor. It must bond to the drain's own membrane flange or clamping ring, because the joint between floor and drain is where many leaks start.

Stone, benches and light in a wet room

A wet floor needs grip. Polished marble is slippery when wet, so underfoot we use honed or textured finishes, or smaller tiles where the extra grout lines help. Terrazzo, porcelain and honed stone can all work well. Dark marble looks striking but shows limescale and soap residue quickly in hard water areas, so we will talk honestly about cleaning before you choose it.

A floating stone bench makes a wet room far more usable. It is built on a rigid, waterproof frame, tanked as part of the wet zone, and topped with stone laid with a slight fall so water runs off rather than sitting. Warm LED light beneath it, or inside a shower niche, uses low-voltage strip in a profile rated for direct exposure to water, with the driver placed outside the wet area where it can be reached without lifting a tile.

For more on choosing, setting out and sealing natural stone, see our stone and tiling page.

Water, ventilation and the regulations

A ceiling rain head needs a good flow of water to feel generous. A combination boiler may struggle to supply one, and a pump can only be used on a gravity-fed system, never connected directly to the mains. We check pressure and flow at survey, then recommend a shower and thermostatic valve that suit your supply rather than one that only looks good in a brochure.

Because the whole room gets wet, extraction matters even more than in a standard bathroom. Part F of the Building Regulations sets a minimum extract rate for bathrooms, and we size and duct the fan to meet it. Underfloor heating helps the floor dry between showers. Electrical work follows the bathroom zone rules and, in England, much of it is notifiable under Part P, so it is certified on completion.

A fixed glass panel is worth considering even in a fully open wet room. Frameless glass keeps the room feeling open while stopping spray reaching towels, the WC and the basin.

Questions

Wet rooms: common questions

Can you put a wet room upstairs?

Often, yes. On a timber floor the shower area is usually lowered between the joists and fitted with a pre-formed former, so the floor finishes level with the rest of the house. It depends on the depth and direction of the joists and the route for the waste pipe to the soil stack. We check all of these at survey and tell you honestly if a walk-in shower would be the better option.

Do wet rooms leak?

A well-built wet room should not. Leaks come from waterproofing that does not cover the whole floor, a poor joint between the membrane and the drain, a floor that moves, or falls that let water sit. Each of these is a build quality problem rather than a flaw in the idea of a wet room. That is why we inspect and water-test the membrane and drain before any tiles go down.

What is the difference between a wet room and a walk-in shower?

In a walk-in shower, water is contained by a low tray and a glass screen, and only that area is waterproofed. In a wet room, the floor itself is the shower tray: it is level throughout, the whole room is tanked and water drains through a floor drain. Wet rooms give true level access and a more open look, while walk-in showers involve less structural work.

Are wet room floors slippery?

They can be if the wrong finish is used. Polished stone and glossy tiles are slippery when wet. We use honed or textured stone, slip-resistant porcelain or smaller tiles in the showering area, where more grout lines add grip. If anyone in your home is less steady on their feet, tell us at survey and we will factor that into the choice of floor and the position of any bench.

Does a wet room need a glass screen?

It does not have to have one, but in most rooms we recommend at least a fixed panel. Without a screen, spray reaches towels, toilet roll and the basin, and the whole floor stays wet for longer. A single frameless panel keeps the open feel while containing most of the water. In a large room with the shower well away from everything else, you may not need one.

How do I look after a marble wet room?

Use a pH-neutral cleaner made for stone and avoid acidic or bleach-based products, which can dull marble. Squeegee or wipe the walls after showering, especially in hard water areas, to keep limescale down. Natural stone is sealed during installation and needs resealing from time to time; how often depends on the stone and how the room is used, and we give you written care guidance at handover.
