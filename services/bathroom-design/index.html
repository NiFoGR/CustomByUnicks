Most bathroom regrets are decided before the first tile

When people are unhappy with a new bathroom, it is rarely the tap or the tile they regret. It is the shower that is a little too tight, the niche on the wrong wall, the strip of cut tile at eye level, or the light that glares off the mirror every morning.

Almost all of those problems were fixed in place before the first tile went on. They come from a layout that was never drawn to scale, a drain put wherever was easiest, or lighting chosen on the day the electrician arrived. By the time you can see them, they are built into the walls.

Design is the stage that stops this happening. It is where the room is worked out properly, on paper and on screen, while changes still cost nothing.

What skipping design really costs

Without a proper design stage, decisions get made on site under time pressure. A fitter has to choose where to start the tiles, how high the niche goes and where the valve sits, often without knowing what you had in mind.

  • Rework. Moving a drain or a valve after the floor is screeded or the wall is boarded means taking work apart.
  • Wrong orders. Stone and large-format porcelain are often ordered in batches. Running short, or ordering the wrong size for the setting out, can mean delays or a slight shade difference.
  • A room that dates quickly. A bathroom without a clear idea behind it tends to collect finishes that do not quite belong together, and it starts to look tired sooner.

A considered design takes time at the start. It saves far more time, money and frustration during the build.

Survey and measure: starting from the real room

Every design starts with a free survey. Plans and estate agent drawings are rarely accurate enough for a bathroom, where a few centimetres decide whether a shower screen fits. We measure the room ourselves, including ceiling heights, window and door positions, and how far the walls are out of square or plumb.

We also look at what you cannot see in a photo. Where is the soil stack, and how much fall can a waste pipe get to it? Which way do the floor joists run, and how stiff is the floor? What is the water pressure and flow like, and what kind of hot water system do you have? These answers decide which showers will perform well, whether a level-access floor is realistic and where the WC can sensibly go.

Many homes in [City] have been altered over the years, so we never assume. If something needs opening up to check, we tell you before it affects the design rather than after.

How you use the room, and the layout options

Before we draw anything, we ask how the room is used. Who uses it, and when? Do you want a bath at all, or would a larger walk-in shower suit you better? Do two people get ready at the same time? Where do towels, toiletries and cleaning things need to live?

From there we prepare two or three layout options drawn to scale. Each one shows clearances in front of the WC and basin, the swing of the door, the shower area and where storage goes. Sometimes the best answer is to move the door or borrow a little space from a cupboard. Sometimes it is to keep the plumbing where it is and spend the budget on the finish instead. We explain the trade-offs plainly so you can decide.

If a wet room or a level shower is on your list, our page on wet room design explains what the floor needs to make that work.

3D visuals: see the room before you order

Once a layout is agreed, we build it as a 3D visual. You see the room from the doorway, from the shower and from the basin, with the stone, timber, brass and light shown together. It is far easier to judge a vanity width or a niche height when you can see it in the room than when it is a number on a drawing.

This is the cheapest point to change your mind. Swapping a tile, moving a niche or trying a different mirror costs a revision, not a rebuild. We would much rather you change things here, as often as you need, than live with something you are unsure about.

A visual is an accurate guide to layout, proportion and finish, but it is not a photograph. Natural stone varies from slab to slab, so we also show you real samples so you can see the colour and veining in daylight.

Setting out, light and drains: the decisions that decide the finish

The details that make a bathroom look precise are fixed early, and they depend on each other. This is the part most homeowners never see, and it is where design earns its keep.

Setting out the tiles

Good tiling follows a plan, in line with BS 5385 practice. We set out from the features you look at most, such as the centre of the vanity, the shower valve or the WC, so cuts are balanced at both sides and no thin slivers land in a corner or at eye level. Niche heights and shelves are aligned to tile courses so the joints run cleanly through them.

Drain positions and falls

In a walk-in shower or wet room, the floor needs a consistent fall to the drain. A linear drain against a wall lets the floor fall in one direction, which suits large-format tiles and stone because they can stay whole. A centre drain needs the floor to fall from all sides, which means more cuts in an envelope pattern. Choosing the drain type and position at design stage decides how the floor will look.

Where the light goes

Lighting has to be planned before the walls are built. LED strips in niches, plinths or benches need channels, cable routes and drivers that stay accessible, not sealed behind stone. Every fitting is placed with the bathroom electrical zones in mind and given the right IP rating for its position. Our bathroom lighting page goes into this in more depth.

Valves and access

Shower valve height, the position of controls so you can turn the water on without getting wet, and access to concealed cisterns and valves are all fixed in the design, so the room is easy to live with and easy to maintain.

Specification, quote and what happens next

The design ends with a full specification: every tile and stone, every fitting and finish, the lighting plan, the drain and valve positions, and the setting out. That document is what the build follows, and it is what our fixed written quote is based on, so there are no gaps for surprises to hide in.

Design & 3D visuals: common questions

Do I need a bathroom designer or can my fitter plan it?

A good fitter can install a layout well, but planning decides things like setting out, drain position, lighting routes and valve height before work starts. Without that stage, those decisions are made on site under time pressure. With one team doing both, the design is drawn by people who know how it will be built, and built by people who know what was intended.

How accurate are 3D bathroom visuals?

They are accurate for layout, proportions, sizes and how the finishes work together, because they are built from the measured survey. They are not a photograph. Natural stone varies between slabs and real light behaves differently in every room, so we also show you physical samples. Use the visual to judge space and balance, and the samples to judge colour and texture.

Can you move the toilet or shower to a different wall?

Often, but it depends on the drainage. A WC needs a large waste pipe with a steady fall to the soil stack, and a shower waste needs enough fall too. Joist direction and floor depth affect what is possible. We check all of this at survey and tell you which moves are straightforward, which need more work, and which are better avoided.

What happens if I change my mind after seeing the design?

That is exactly what the design stage is for. Changing a tile, moving a niche or trying a different vanity on screen costs a revision, not a rebuild. We would rather you take the time to get it right before anything is ordered. Once the specification is signed off and materials are ordered, changes become harder and may affect the price and timing.

Why does tile setting out matter so much?

Setting out decides where the cuts fall. Done well, tiles are centred on the features you look at, with balanced cuts at each side and joints that line up with niches and shelves. Done badly, you get thin slivers in corners and at eye level, which is usually what makes a bathroom look amateur even when the tiles are expensive. It has to be planned before tiling starts.

Do you plan the lighting as part of the design?

Yes. Lighting in niches, plinths, benches and mirrors needs cable routes, channels and accessible drivers built in from the start. Each fitting is chosen for its position in the room's electrical zones with a suitable IP rating. Planning it during design means the light lands where you want it and can be maintained later without breaking tiles.
